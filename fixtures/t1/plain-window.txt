In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was
God. John does not start with a manger. He starts with eternity. The church
has spent centuries learning not to say less than that sentence. The Son is
not a creature who happened to be unusually obedient. The Word was God, and
the Word became flesh and dwelt among us. That movement — from eternal speech
to a particular human life — is the scandal and the comfort of Christmas
preaching in every century.

Creeds try to protect that confession when the questions of the day threaten
it. They are not replacements for Scripture. They are emergency fences. The
Nicene fathers were answering Arius, not writing a systematic theology
textbook for their own amusement. When we recite “God from God, light from
light, true God from true God, begotten not made,” we are refusing a smaller
Jesus. We are also refusing to pretend that fourth-century Greek substance
language is the same thing as John’s own emphasis.

John’s own emphasis is revelation. No one has ever seen God. The only Son,
who is himself God and is at the Father’s side, has made him known. The point
is not that we can diagram the inner life of God with geometric precision.
The point is that we can know the Father because the Son has shown him.
Philip says, show us the Father and it will be enough. Jesus answers, whoever
has seen me has seen the Father. Trinitarian theology that never arrives at
that sentence has taken a wrong turn.

Israel already knew that God speaks. The law came through Moses, words on
stone, a gift that showed the people what their God is like. The tragedy of
the Old Testament is not that the law was harsh. The tragedy is that the
firstborn son abandoned the word. The prophets promised a fresh word, a
clearer unveiling of glory. John says that word has become a person. Grace
and truth are no longer only inscribed. They walk, eat, wash feet, and go to
a cross.

This is why orphan language matters. If we do not know the Father we are lost
in the world, however religious our vocabulary. The Son’s work is to bring us
home and then to send us to do the Father’s works. Jesus tells the disciples
they will do greater works — not a promise of celebrity miracles, but a
promise that the knowledge of the Father will bear fruit in obedience among
the nations. Abstract speculation about essence can wait. The T1 index needs
this plain prose chunked so “Word became flesh,” “Nicene,” and “greater
works” can be found without paying for a decompose.

Pastoral application is ordinary. Read John 1 slowly. Refuse a Jesus who is
only an example. Refuse a theology that never becomes prayer. Ask whether
your picture of the Father looks like the Son. If it looks like a crank, or
like an indifferent manager, you are not looking at Jesus. The light shines
in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it. That is enough light
to walk by until a later tier adds rhetorical functions, doctrinal loci, and
citation graphs. For unpaid bulk ingest, this windowed text is the whole
point: durable id, offsets, and a keyword handle on the sermon.
