Tools For Transformation // Ephesians 4:17-32
Introduction: Tools allow us to be more productive. But there’s a learning curve for every tool. And so some people, who lack patience, stop trying to learn the tool – and just do the work in a less efficient, but more intuitive way.
I want to give you essential tools for transformation. But I need two weeks to do it. And I need you to spend some time through the week thinking about all of this. 
Now this I say and testify in the Lord, that you must no longer walk as the Gentiles do, in the futility of their minds. They are darkened in their understanding, alienated from the life of God because of the ignorance that is in them, due to their hardness of heart. They have become callous and have given themselves up to sensuality, greedy to practice every kind of impurity. But that is not the way you learned Christ!— assuming that you have heard about him and were taught in him, as the truth is in Jesus, to put off your old self, which belongs to your former manner of life and is corrupt through deceitful desires, and to be renewed in the spirit of your minds, and to put on the new self, created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness. 
Therefore, having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor, for we are members one of another. Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil. Let the thief no longer steal, but rather let him labor, doing honest work with his own hands, so that he may have something to share with anyone in need. Let no corrupting talk come out of your mouths, but only such as is good for building up, as fits the occasion, that it may give grace to those who hear. And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, by whom you were sealed for the day of redemption. Let all bitterness and wrath and anger and clamor and slander be put away from you, along with all malice. Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you.
A few broad observations before we enter the passage proper.
First, attend to that word must. "You must no longer walk as the Gentiles do." Paul is not offering a suggestion. He is not saying, "It might be a good idea if you stopped living like pagans." He says you must not. This is an imperative — but it is an imperative rooted in something already accomplished. The new creation is not a proposal put before committee. It is a new nature, and it carries a new obligation.
Second, we have here yet another instance of a principle that runs through the whole of this letter: the interior state is the engine of outward conduct.
Consider how Paul prays in the first and third chapters. He does not pray, "Lord, make them behave better." He prays that they would know — know the hope of their calling, know the riches of their inheritance, know the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge. He returns again and again to what has been done for us, to us, in us. The entire premise of Ephesians is that what is happening inside a man is what finally matters. Behaviour is downstream of being.
And here we are given the negative illustration.
Look at verses twenty-five through thirty-one. Paul wants the Ephesians to put off certain things and put on certain things. Falsehood is to give way to truth-telling. Theft is to give way to honest labour and generosity. Corrupt speech is to give way to words that build up and communicate grace. Bitterness, wrath, anger, clamour, slander, and malice are to give way to kindness, tenderheartedness, and forgiveness.
But notice — Paul does not begin with the list. He begins in verses seventeen through twenty-four with the internal condition that produces the behaviour. The put-offs and put-ons are downstream. The source is the heart.
Here is what that interior state looks like in the unregenerate man, as Paul describes it in those earlier verses:
Futility of mind — the thinking produces nothing of lasting value; the engine is running but the wheels are not gripping the road. 
Darkened understanding — not merely ignorant, but actively darkened; the lamp has gone out. 
Alienation from the life of God — severed from the source of true life, as a branch cut from the vine. 
Ignorance rooted in hardness of heart — and this is not the innocent ignorance of someone who simply hasn't heard; the hard heart is what causes the blindness. 
A conscience past feeling, calloused — the spiritual nerves have been so thoroughly deadened that pain no longer registers. 
Surrender to sensuality — having handed themselves over, they are now carried along by whatever appetite presents itself. 
And finally, a greed for every kind of impurity — insatiable, because the appetite grows with feeding; it never satisfies.
This is the operating system of the unregenerate man. And this is precisely why Paul cannot simply issue commands and consider the matter settled. You cannot repair this list with a programme of behaviour modification. What is needed is a new interior altogether.



The Indicative and the Imperative
This brings us to the third observation, and it is perhaps the most important. Identity is one of the central issues in the Christian life. It is never sufficient to say "stop doing this and start doing that," because this and that flow from something deeper — they flow from what a man is.
Look at verses twenty through twenty-four. The key concept is unmistakable: old self and new self.
But that is not the way you learned Christ!— assuming that you have heard about him and were taught in him, as the truth is in Jesus, to put off your old self, which belongs to your former manner of life and is corrupt through deceitful desires, and to be renewed in the spirit of your minds, and to put on the new self, created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness.
If you were to survey the whole of the New Testament's ethical material — every command, every exhortation, every put-off and put-on — you would find that the imperatives are always rooted in indicatives of identity. And the indicatives tend to move in two directions:
First: who you are. 
Second: whose you are.
Sometimes these appear in the negative, as they do in our passage — "you are not Gentiles; do not walk as Gentiles." But I cannot think of a single imperative passage in the New Testament that does not flow from a prior declaration of identity.
Consider the evidence across the letters.
Second Corinthians
Under the heading of who you are — new creation identity — Paul writes to the Corinthians in his second letter, chapter five, verse seventeen: "If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away; behold, the new has come." And the imperative of ambassadorship in verse twenty flows directly from that declaration. 
Colossians
To the Colossians, chapter three, verses three and five: "You have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God" — and only then, "Put to death therefore what is earthly in you." 
First Peter
Peter writes in his first letter, chapter two, verses nine and eleven: "You are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession" — and from that identity comes the appeal: "I urge you as sojourners and exiles to abstain from the passions of the flesh." 
Galatians
Paul compresses the whole pattern into a single sentence in Galatians chapter two, verse twenty: "I have been crucified with Christ. It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me."
Romans
Under the heading of who you are not — negation — Romans chapter twelve, verse two: "Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind." You are not the world; act accordingly. 
First Peter
Peter again, in his first letter, chapter one, verse fourteen: "As obedient children, do not be conformed to the passions of your former ignorance." The former ignorance is the past; do not take up residence there. 
Colossians
Colossians chapter three, verses nine and ten: "Do not lie to one another, seeing that you have put off the old self with its practices and have put on the new self." The putting-off has already occurred; the imperative flows from the accomplished fact.
First Corinthians
And under the heading of whose you are — belonging and indwelling — Paul to the Corinthians, first letter, chapter six, verses nineteen and twenty: "Do you not know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God? You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body." You belong to Another; the ethics of belonging follow. 
Galatians
Galatians chapter five, verse twenty-five: "If we live by the Spirit, let us also keep in step with the Spirit." You are Spirit-inhabited; walk that way. 
John
And our Lord himself in John chapter fifteen, verses four and five: "Abide in me, and I in you. Whoever abides in me and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit." The union is the source of the fruit — not the reverse.
Every one of these passages moves in the same direction. Who you are and whose you are produces what you do. The imperative always follows the indicative.



The Trinitarian Architecture
Now I want to draw attention to one more structural feature of considerable importance.
Back in chapter one, we observed that salvation was accomplished through the work of the Trinity. The Father elects — that is verses four through six. The Son redeems — verses seven through twelve. The Spirit seals — verses thirteen and fourteen. And at the close of each movement, Paul says it was accomplished "to the praise of his glorious grace."
Salvation is the joint work of Father, Son, and Spirit — for the praise of his glory.
And so it is with sanctification. Paul tells us not to walk as the Gentiles. He then exposes the heart of the pagan in contrast to the heart of the regenerate man. And he founds the entire argument on the same trinitarian architecture:
The Son (20)
"That is not the way you learned Christ" 
The Father (24)
"Put on the new self, created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness" 
The Holy Spirit (30)
"Do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, by whom you were sealed for the day of redemption" 
Chapter one: salvation is rooted in the triune God. 
Chapter four: sanctification is rooted in the triune God. The same three Persons, the same structure, applied differently.
The Godhead, all three persons of the trinity are, in both cases, working together both to save and sanctify. But in the sanctification efforts, another person appears as well – namely you. 
From Monergism to Synergism
But notice the difference between the two movements. In the first — salvation — the process is described as something that happened to us. That is the whole arc of chapter two. We were dead. We did not cooperate. We did not contribute. We were raised while we were still corpses.
In the second — sanctification — the language shifts. It becomes participatory.
You can trace this shift within chapter two itself. In verse one, we are dead — following, almost like zombies, the dark trinity of the world, the flesh, and the devil. 
Then we are raised in Christ. And by the close of the chapter, Paul is describing something that includes our active involvement. 
Chapter two, verses eight through ten: 
"By grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them."
Salvation: a gift. Something done to us. Sanctification: good works God prepared — that we should walk in them. Something done with us.
One way to understand this is to attend carefully to the dynamics at work in chapter four, verses twenty through twenty-four. Paul says: 
"That is not the way you learned Christ — assuming that you have heard about him and were taught in him, as the truth is in Jesus, to put off your old self, which belongs to your former manner of life and is corrupt through deceitful desires, and to be renewed in the spirit of your minds, and to put on the new self, created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness."
Prior to conversion, a man had one self — bound to follow the world, the flesh, and the devil. After conversion, he has, in a certain sense, two selves — the old and the new. A real choice has emerged. Options exist where none existed before.
The Dutch theologian Hulsbosch put it this way: "There is a freedom which man loses in sin, the freedom to direct himself in his own power to God, for all of him is inclined toward evil…”
The theological terms for this distinction are familiar. Salvation is monergistic — God alone does it, to us. Sanctification is synergistic — God does it with us.
This does not mean our efforts originate from ourselves — they operate out of the new man. Nor does it mean that God and I are shouldering equal loads, as two men carrying a wardrobe up a staircase. It is more a matter of conceptual framing than a precise metaphysical claim. 
But it matters enormously — because if you are trying to make progress in holiness, you must not imagine that merely "letting go and letting God" will get you there.
This is rather well clarified by something D.A. Carson says on Second Peter chapter one, verses three through seven. 
The passage reads: "His divine power has granted to us all things that pertain to life and godliness, through the knowledge of him who called us to his own glory and excellence, by which he has granted to us his precious and very great promises, so that through them you may become partakers of the divine nature, having escaped from the corruption that is in the world because of sinful desire. For this very reason, make every effort to supplement your faith with virtue, and virtue with knowledge, and knowledge with self-control, and self-control with steadfastness, and steadfastness with godliness, and godliness with brotherly affection, and brotherly affection with love."
Notice the connection. God supplies everything we need — and then, in verse five, "for this very reason, make every effort."
Carson puts it plainly:
Because God has given us everything we need for life and godliness, including a new nature that enables us to escape the corruption in this world caused by evil desires — for this reason, therefore, make every effort to add to your faith goodness, and to goodness, knowledge, and so forth.
In other words, precisely because God has given us his promises and because his power does work in us — therefore, work at it. The Bible simply does not encourage quietism. "Let go and let God" — it just doesn't. There is a sense in which you may say that sort of thing in order to emphasise that it is God who is doing the work in you. But it is far more common to read in Scripture that because God is doing the work in you, therefore work your little head off.
The text in Philippians does not say, "Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling because God has done his bit and now it's up to you." Nor does it say, "Don't work at it — just trust God and let go and he'll take over and give you everything." It says, "Work out your own salvation because it is God working in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure."



What We Have Established
Let me gather the threads.
	1	Sanctification is not optional. The word "must" in verse seventeen is not a polite suggestion. The new creation carries new obligation.
	2	The interior drives the exterior. Paul's whole method — including his prayers — is to work on the inside. Behaviour is always downstream of being.
	3	The pagan interior is a cascade of spiritual ruin. Futile mind, darkened understanding, alienation, ignorance rooted in hardness, calloused conscience, surrender to sensuality, insatiable appetite for impurity. You cannot repair this sequence with willpower.
	4	Every imperative in the New Testament is rooted in an indicative of identity. You are never simply told to do something. You are told to act in accordance with what you are and with whom you belong to. New creation. Chosen. Bought. Indwelt. The command always follows the declaration.
	5	The two dimensions of sanctification are "who you are" and "whose you are." These correspond to the new self — identity — and the indwelling Spirit — belonging. Both are necessary. Neither alone is Paul's method.
	6	Salvation is trinitarian in chapter one. Sanctification is trinitarian in chapter four. Same three Persons, same structure, different application.
	7	Salvation is monergistic. Sanctification is synergistic. God alone raises the dead. But God works with the living. The shift from the death language of chapter two to the participatory language of chapter four is deliberate.
	8	"Let go and let God" is not in the Bible. The opposite is nearer the truth. Because God is at work in you, therefore work. The indicative of divine action is the fuel for human effort, not a replacement of it.



How Transformation Actually Happens: Three Practices
We have established that sanctification is synergistic — God works, and we work with him. But what does our side of the work actually look like? I want to suggest three things.
Beholding
The Christian life is fundamentally imitative. This is not a secondary feature or an optional extra — it is woven into the very fabric of Paul's language. "Learn Christ." "Be imitators of God." "Walk in love, as Christ loved us." The pattern recurs because the mechanism is real: we become what we behold.
This is, in fact, how human beings are made. We are mimetic creatures. Long before anyone consciously decides to imitate another person, the imitation has already begun. Children absorb the emotional register of their parents before they can speak. Athletes unconsciously mirror the mechanics of those they admire. Apprentices acquire the habits, the posture, the instincts of their masters without a single explicit instruction.
The Christian life operates on the same principle. Paul tells the Corinthians, in his second letter, chapter three, verse eighteen: "We all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another." The transformation is happening in or through the beholding. 
I learned something about this on a motorcycle. When you are riding — particularly through a curve — every instructor drills the same principle into you: where you look is where you go. If you are coming into a bend and you fix your eyes on the guardrail, you will ride into the guardrail. The motorcycle follows your gaze. And the correction is not to wrestle the handlebars away from the danger. The correction is to look where you want to go. Fix your eyes on the exit of the curve, and the machine follows almost of its own accord.
The Christian life works in precisely the same way. The remedy for sin is rarely to stare harder at the sin. It is to redirect the gaze — consistently, deliberately — toward Christ. The transformation follows the attention. This is why Paul's prayers in chapters one and three are prayers for knowing — knowing the hope, knowing the love, knowing the fullness of God. He is praying that the eyes of their hearts would be fixed on the right object. Behold Christ, and the motorcycle goes where you are looking.
This is also why identity affirmations are relatively futile. You might think that the way to sanctification comes via constant reminding yourself of who you are in Christ and whose you are in Christ. But we do not become like someone by saying we are like someone. We become like someone by studying them and spending time with them. 
Prayer for Transformation
Paul does not merely teach about transformation. He prays for it. Twice in this letter he pauses to intercede, and it is worth attending to what he actually asks.
In chapter one, he prays for the knowledge of God — that they would receive a spirit of wisdom and revelation, that the eyes of their hearts would be opened to perceive what is theirs in Christ. In chapter three, he prays for power and love — that Christ would dwell in their hearts through faith, that they would be rooted and grounded in love, that they would come to know the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge, and be filled with all the fullness of God.
These are not prayers for willpower. They are not prayers for stronger resolve or improved habits. They are prayers for more of God — more seeing, more knowing, more love, more of the indwelling presence. Paul understood that what we cannot manufacture within ourselves, we may receive through prayer. The Spirit who indwells us is also the Spirit we may ask to work more deeply within us. That is not passivity. It is, in truth, the most active thing a creature can do before its Creator.
Making Structural Changes — The Wisdom of a Son
Here is something rather interesting. When Paul addresses the thief in verse twenty-eight, he says: "Let the thief no longer steal, but rather let him labour, doing honest work with his own hands, so that he may have something to share with anyone in need."
That is the command. What Paul does not do is explain how to find employment. He says nothing about how to write a letter of introduction, acquire a trade, approach a potential employer, or manage one's wages once they begin coming in. He simply says: work, so that you may give.
But the thing is — if the thief does not sort out those practical matters, he cannot obey. There is a vast layer of practical wisdom and structural action that Paul leaves entirely in the hands of the believer. He supplies the destination. The route is yours to navigate.
And this is the freedom and the responsibility of a son, as distinct from a slave. A slave requires every step to be managed. A son understands his father's purposes and has both the liberty and the obligation to work out the particulars. 
You want to break a pattern of anger? You may need to identify your triggers, restructure certain relationships, sleep more, stop drinking, find a wise counsellor. Paul is not going to prescribe those specifics. But none of it happens without them.
Beholding gives you the right direction. Prayer keeps the power flowing. Structural wisdom works out how to actually arrive.
All three. Together. That is the walk.
