HEALING THE SICK

Savior and Teacher: A Study of Matthew—March 4, 2012

Matthew 8:5–17

  5 When Jesus had entered Capernaum, a centurion came to him, asking for help. 6 “Lord,” he said, “my servant lies at home paralyzed, and in terrible suffering.” 7 Jesus said to him, “I will go and heal him.”
  8 The centurion replied, “Lord, I do not deserve to have you come under my roof. But just say the word, and my servant will be healed. 9 For I myself am a man under authority, with soldiers under me. I tell this one, ‘Go,’ and he goes; and that one, ‘Come,’ and he comes. I say to my servant, ‘Do this,’ and he does it.”
  10 When Jesus heard this, he was astonished and said to those following him, “I tell you the truth, I have not found anyone in Israel with such great faith. 11 I say to you that many will come from the east and the west, and will take their places at the feast with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven. 12 But the subjects of the kingdom will be thrown outside, into the darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.”
  13 Then Jesus said to the centurion, “Go! It will be done just as you believed it would.” And his servant was healed at that very hour. 14 When Jesus came into Peter’s house, he saw Peter’s mother-in-law lying in bed with a fever. 15 He touched her hand and the fever left her, and she got up and began to wait on him.
  16 When evening came, many who were demon-possessed were brought to him, and he drove out the spirits with a word and healed all the sick. 17 This was to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah: “He took up our infirmities and carried our diseases.”

  The Word of the Lord



In these weeks up to Easter we’re looking at the life of Jesus through the gospel of Matthew. When you look at the life of Jesus you are always struck, if you ever read through it, by the number of his miracles. What I’ve done is chosen a fairly generic passage. In fact, there’s a healing just before in 8:1–4, there is the healing of a centurion servant, there are healings afterwards, and there are many other healings in the book of Matthew.
What I want to do just briefly today is to give you what I would call a high-level way of understanding the miracles of Christ, because if you don’t understand the miracles of Christ you don’t understand him. The reason it’s important to talk about it is the only other people we know with supernatural, miraculous power are fictional figures. They’re either figures in ancient legends or modern fiction.
In ancient legends, Merlin. In modern fiction, superheroes … Superman. They have non-natural powers. The have supernatural powers. But I want to show you in the stories of the world, the supernatural, miraculous powers always work in a completely different way than they work in Jesus’ life, and that tells us a lot about Jesus. Jesus’ miracles are proof, pointer, and pattern. They are part of the proof of who he is, they’re a pointer to where he wants to take the world, and they’re a deep pattern of how he comes to save us.


1. Jesus’ miracles are part of the proof of who he is

You see (not just here) at the very end, in verse 17, the miracles fulfill some Old Testament prophecies about the Messiah. Over and over you’ll see places in the Bible where it says, “He did this and he did this, and his disciples believed in him.” Therefore, obviously, one of the reasons Jesus does these great miracles is to show the world he’s the divine Son of God, to show his greatness.
The centurion here, interestingly enough, tells us a little bit about how great he must be, because the centurion says, “I am a man under authority.” What he means by that is, “I have been given a certain number of soldiers. Inside the sphere of authority I have been given, my word is law.” In other words, “I have absolute power inside my sphere of authority. I say something and they do it.”
But then he says, “So you don’t even need to come to my home.” What is he saying? He is saying, “The whole world is the sphere of your authority.” The centurion knows Jesus isn’t a miracle worker who has to get in the room and say, “Abracadabra,” and do all the stuff you see in legends. The whole world is the sphere of his authority. The centurion says, “All you have to do is say it. All you have to do is think it, and he will be healed.” So the miracles are a way of showing us part of the evidence of who he is.
Now, I need to do a little sidebar. I have to, because when I was first coming on as a Christian (quite a while ago, in the 60s and 70s), one of the very main reasons many people said Jesus can’t be real and these things must be legends is because of the miracles. On my college campus, one of the main reasons people couldn’t believe in Christianity they said was because MCH … Miracles Can’t Happen.
Are you aware of the fact that things have changed? That is not as big an objection as it used to be. In fact, one of the ways we found that out was I did a Question and Answer time. I used to do Question and Answer times after the morning and evening services. We had two services over at Hunter College.
I remember one time when I was preaching on John 6 and Jesus feeding the 5,000, and I said, “If you believe miracles can’t happen (MCH) I want to see you right after the service. I’m ready for you. I’m loaded for you. I want to talk to you about this.” I was all ready, and very few people came. This was like 1997. In fact, the only people who came were men over the age of 45, and I began to realize there was a certain kind of person who still felt miracles cannot happen. But for various reasons, that’s a minority opinion in the world today. Do you know that?
If you feel like miracles can’t happen, you might be a little bit irritated by my saying so, but it’s a fact. There are a couple of reasons why. Here are two possible positions for you if you say miracles can’t happen. First, you can either say, “I believe in God, and miracles can’t happen.” Wait a minute. That’s a non sequitur. If there’s a God, it would be perfectly reasonable to believe in miracles. How could miracles not happen if there’s a God?
“Okay,” you say, “I don’t believe in God, and miracles cannot happen.” Okay, but let me be a little mischievous here. You have heard of quantum mechanics and chaos theory, which say now (in the last 20–30 years) we’ve learned that at the atomic and subatomic levels the so-called laws of nature we think are iron-clad laws don’t work. Therefore, the universe is much more open than we think. In other words, if there is no God anything can happen. Anything has happened. How can you say you don’t believe in miracles?
Here’s my point. To say miracles cannot happen is a leap of faith. It’s a dogmatic, a priori assumption, and I don’t want you to be so dogmatic you can’t stand back and see the real evidence for the reality and truth of Christianity is the full portrait of Jesus. How did this centurion know Jesus could do this? He had never seen Jesus work like this. How did he know? Jesus is even astonished.
The point is he had been contemplating. He had been looking at the totality. He had been looking at Jesus’ claims of deity. He had been looking at Jesus’ life. He had been looking at the way he deals with people. He had been looking at the power of Jesus and the sweetness of Jesus. He’d come to the conclusion, “This must be the Son of God,” and that is the most compelling argument for Christianity. It is to look at every part of who Jesus is and to say, “How could all this come together in one person?”
A friend of mine, Dick Lucas, once talked to a skeptic who said, “If I had a watertight argument for the existence of God I’d believe.” He came back and said, “What if God didn’t give us a watertight argument for his existence but a watertight person, Jesus Christ, against whom in the end there can be no argument?” What he means by that is the sum total of everything about Jesus, including his miracles, is compelling evidence he really is the Son of God. Don’t let a dogmatic leap of faith keep you from at least considering and contemplating the totality of who he is.


2. Jesus’ miracles are a pointer to where he wants to take the world

Let’s again contrast Jesus’ supernatural, miraculous power with some of the figures we know. Jesus’ power cannot only be a demonstration of his power. Why? If so, he would do things like Merlin and Superman. Here’s what Superman can do. He says, “You don’t believe I’m Superman? Watch. I’ll blow the top of that mountain off with my x-ray vision,” and you’ll say, “Yo, you really are Superman.”
But you never see Jesus doing stuff like that. You never see him blowing the tops off of mountains or things like that (which would be perfectly fine). Why doesn’t he do that? Because the miracles are not simply naked displays of power pointing to him. They’re actually pointing to where he wants the world to go. Essentially all Jesus’ miracles deal with the suffering of the human race.
I’m paraphrasing, but B.B. Warfield, a biblical scholar, says, “The number of the miracles Jesus Christ did may easily be underrated. It has been said that, in effect, he banished disease and death from Palestine for three years. If this is exaggeration, it is pardonable exaggeration. For wherever he went he brought a blessing. We greatly underestimate his beneficent activity as he went about feeding the hungry, healing the sick and the blind and deaf, liberating the oppressed, raising the dead, and as Luke said, going everywhere, doing good.”
Jesus’ miracles were not just naked displays of power. They were dealing with human suffering. That means they pointed. Where? They point back (way back) and ahead (way ahead). Do you realize where they’re pointing? They’re pointing way back to the way the world was when God first made it, the way the world was when it was the way God wanted it to be.
When he feeds the hungry, he’s pointing us back to a time in which there were no little children with swollen bellies dying of starvation. When he heals the sick and the leper and raises the dead, he’s pointing back to a world in which there was no suffering, disease, or death. When he stills the storm, he’s pointing to a time in which nature was our friend, not our enemy, and we weren’t always dying because of blizzards, avalanches, earthquakes, and tsunamis.
He’s pointing back to the way the world was before sin and our turning away from God, the true ruler of the world, broke everything apart. Therefore (as we’ve said before), you must understand Jesus’ miracles are not primarily suspensions of natural laws. They are the restoration of natural law! Death, decay, and suffering are suspensions of God’s original natural order, and Jesus Christ in his miracles is putting it back, at least temporarily. That’s why one theologian essentially says, “Jesus’ healings are the only natural thing in a world that is unnatural, demonic, and wounded.”
Then, of course, Jesus’ miracles are not just pointing way back. They’re pointing way ahead. He even mentions the messianic feast. Verse 11 says, “I say to you that many will come from the east and the west, and will take their places at the feast with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob …” He’s talking about something the Old Testament prophets and the book of Revelation talk about. That is, someday when God comes back to really rule, Psalm 96 says, “Then will the trees of the woods sing for joy. Then will the sea roar and sing for joy. For he comes to rule the earth.”
When Christ comes back to rule the earth he will heal everything, and it will be like a feast. No more sickness. No more decay. No more crime. No more war. No more suffering. No more death. No more tears. That means Jesus’ miracles prove he is no happier with the way things are than you and I. That means we have a radical agenda.
Jesus’ miracles give us a radical agenda. What are they? Listen very carefully, because I’m trying to nuance what I’m about to say. When Jesus Christ shows he is not happy with the way things are, that he, even now, temporarily, is dealing with the suffering, injustice, disease, and hunger through his miracles, it’s a foretaste of what he’s going to do eventually.
What that means is while we’re here, we too need to have just as much holy dissatisfaction with the injustice, brokenness, sickness, and suffering we see out there, and we want to do something about it. Christianity is a fighting religion. C.S. Lewis says when we see cancer or a slum, we say that ought not to be, and we do what we can. In fact, Jesus actually says in John 14, “Whoever believes in me will do the works I do, and even greater.”
If you think that means miraculously dealing with suffering, no. We’re not going to do greater, because you can even look in the book of Acts, and you’ll see even though his disciples did a few miracles they were nothing like what Jesus could do. We should never think that’s what he means.
What he means is, “A billion people who are working off the same agenda out there can do greater works than even I do.” Here’s what I mean by nuance. Do you see (and we don’t have the time to go into this) over and over again when Jesus does a miracle he is concerned about the faith of the person he is helping?
When the woman with the hemorrhage touches his garment and she’s healed, he insists she come out and profess her faith. If you look carefully (and if we had time to go through this) you’ll see he actually pushes back a bit on the centurion. He’s trying to say, “What is it you want me to do? Do you want me to go and heal him?” He’s concerned to see the centurion’s faith.
Here’s what that means. The greatest thing you can do for anybody is not heal their body. It’s to give them a permanent and, therefore, eternal relationship with God through Jesus Christ. That’s the greatest thing you can do for anyone you love. Faith. Jesus shows us that’s the most important thing, not making the world a better place. And yet, he ministers in word and deed. He seeks to get people to believe in him, and yet, as you can see, there’s a certain indiscriminateness. You can see that down in verses 16 and 17. At some points he just heals people.
That’s how we need to be in this neighborhood. On the one hand, we need to respectfully and humbly call our neighbors to pay attention to Jesus, and to say, “Consider his claims and who he is, and see what spiritual riches he can give you should you believe in him. See what it means to be reconciled to the Father through what Jesus Christ has done.”
On the other hand we say, “But we are going to do what we can to alleviate need and suffering. We’re going to do what we can to love our neighbors whether they believe like we do or not,” because that’s how Jesus did it. His miracles are a radical agenda of caring for the needs of this world at the same time as we’re seeking to help people believe in him. Do you see how different they are?


3. Jesus’ miracles are a deep pattern of how he comes to save us

I’ll show you one more way in which the miracles are very different. The miracles are so different than the supernatural power of fictional figures. They not only alleviate suffering, but they do something else. Jesus’ miracles show the deep pattern of how he saved us.
Do you know how Satan tempted Jesus? If you were here a couple weeks ago when we looked at Matthew 4, you know two of the temptations, where Satan says, “Jesus, use your supernatural power to turn these stones into bread. You’re hungry, aren’t you? Just snap your fingers and … Bread!” There’s another place where he says, “Throw yourself off this cliff or pinnacle and come down without harming yourself and show the world who you are.”
What’s he doing? Isn’t this interesting? He’s trying to say, “I want you to be like Merlin. I want you to be like Superman,” because the supernatural powers of Merlin and Superman made them invulnerable. They made them impossible to attack. You couldn’t spear Superman. You couldn’t nail Superman. You couldn’t kill Superman, because his supernatural power made him invulnerable.
Jesus’ supernatural power does not do that. The grand miracle was the incarnation in which the Son of God became vulnerable. When the woman with the hemorrhage touched Jesus’ garment, do you remember what happened? How did he know she had been healed? He said, “Power went out from me.” He felt weak. In other words, his strength went to her. Her weakness went to him. Isn’t that interesting?
Do you know how often when Jesus Christ did a miracle it made his enemies angrier at him? Finally when he raised Lazarus from the dead in John 11, his enemies said, “Now we have to kill him.” Jesus’ supernatural power not only did not make him invulnerable, it made him more vulnerable, more spearable, more nailable, and more killable. Why?
The very last verse tells you. How does Jesus deal with our brokenness? He bears it! Because if Jesus Christ had actually come to earth ready to judge all evil and destroy all the evil that was destroying the world … If Jesus Christ came to bring judgment, we all would be gone. Why? Because you know it’s in us. You know we’re part of the problem. You know evil is in us. You know that.
If Jesus Christ just came to bring judgment we’d be gone, but he came to bear the judgment. He bore it! Not just the diseases but all the brokenness. Death itself. All the curse of us turning away from God. The disintegration, the weakness, the suffering, and the death that is our lot now fell on him on the cross. He bore the judgment so someday he can come back and make the world perfect again and end all evil without ending you and me.
The miraculous power shows that basically the deep pattern is strength through weakness. Jesus Christ saves us by becoming weak, going to the cross and dying for us. You and I get the power of God in our lives. How? By becoming weak, by repenting, by saying, “I need a Savior,” by saying, “I need forgiveness,” by saying, “I need a Master and a Lord.”
How are we going to bless the country? How are we going to bless the neighborhood? How are we going to bless the world? How are we going to deal with cancer and slums? We have to become weak, because the only way to really pour yourself out and to do the great works Jesus did is to give your money away, to give your time away, and to give your heart away. That’s what we have to do.
Rodney Stark, a great historian who wrote a great book called The Rise of Christianity, about why Christianity succeeded in the early centuries in the Roman world, tells us one of the reasons was because when plagues went through the great cities of the Greco-Roman Empire, people were literally dying in the street. The first one happened in AD 165. Another one happened a century later. Most people just stopped taking care of people and headed for the hills, but the Christians did not.
Let me give you two eyewitness accounts. I’m paraphrasing, but the first one says, “The doctors were quite incapable of treating the disease. The people became afraid to visit anyone, and as a result, thousands of people died with no one to look after them. Indeed, there were many houses in which all of the inhabitants perished through lack of any attention.
The bodies of the dying were heaped, one on top of the other, and half-dead creatures could be seen staggering about in the streets.” What a nightmare. “The catastrophe was so overwhelming that men became indifferent to every rule of morality. Many pushed sufferers away, even their dearest, often throwing them into the road before they were dead, hoping to avert contagion.”
But that’s not how the Christians acted. The Christians stayed and took care of not only their own sick but the sick of the people who weren’t Christians. Here’s what happened. I’m paraphrasing again, but this is another eyewitness account:
“Most Christians in the plague showed unbounded love and loyalty, never sparing themselves and only thinking of others. Heedless of danger, they took charge of the sick, attended to their every need, ministering to them in Christ, and many departed their life serenely happy, for they were infected by their neighbors and cheerfully accepted their pains. The best of our brothers lost their lives in this manner, a number of elders. Many in nursing and curing others transferred their death to themselves and died in their stead.”
Where do you think they got that idea from? What would empower people to be that weak? Of course, Rodney Stark’s whole point is when Christians did that, you realize what enormous power and credibility the gospel message had after that. Those people died, but there was a resurrection. Those people were weak, but there was enormous power in what they did.
Those people were following the pattern of Jesus, and that’s what we should be doing in this neighborhood. That’s what we should be doing in this city. Do you see how the miraculous power of Jesus is so utterly different than the stories of the world? Think of that. Go and do likewise. Let us pray.

Our Father, we pray you’d help us now as we come to the Table to internalize what we’ve just learned with our heads. We pray you would help us to understand better what your Son did for us and what this radical agenda is now for us to live out, here in this neighborhood, here in this city, in our lives in this world. We pray this in Jesus’ name, amen.
